Learning from examples

The number that worked until it didn't

Every step is the slope times one small number. Set that number a little too high and learning does not slow down. It runs away.

The program on the previous page learned km per mile with one rule: step against the slope, by 0.0005 of it. On seven logged trips it went from a guess of 1 to 1.609 in thirteen steps.

Then someone takes the car on a long drive and logs it: 150 miles, 241.4 km. The reading is honest, and 241.4 ÷ 150 is 1.609. Add it to the log and run the same program from the same guess.

The guess passes 1.609 on the first step and lands further away every time after. By the fifth step the program claims a mile is 35 kilometres, and the loss has grown from about 1,200 to 3.6 million. Nothing in the rule changed, and the new trip is not a mistake.

The 0.0005 has a name. It is the learning rate, usually written η, the Greek letter eta, and every step is the learning rate times the slope:

new w = w − η × slope

That reads: move against the slope, by η of it. The name suggests a dial for how fast the program learns, and up to a point it is one. A run settles when its steps shrink toward nothing, as they did on the previous page, and blows up when they grow, as they do in the table. This page finds the rate where one turns into the other, and shows why one more trip moved it.

Turn the dial yourself

Go back to the seven trips, where 0.0005 worked. Below, you set η and take the steps. As before, you see what the program sees: the current guess, its loss and its slope.

On the previous page each step covered about 47% of the distance to the answer. Here is where the 47% comes from. For these trips the slope is 948 times the guess's distance from 1.609. Call that 948 the steepness of the loss: how much slope there is for each unit of distance from the answer. A step of η × slope is then a step of 948 × η × distance, and what is left afterwards is:

distance after = (1 − 948 × η) × distance before

That reads: every step multiplies the distance by the same number, whatever the guess is. Call it the multiplier. The previous page's 0.0005 gives a multiplier of 0.53: each step leaves 53% of the distance, which is the other 47% covered. Drag the rate to see others: at η = the multiplier is .

A larger rate gives a smaller multiplier and a faster run. At 0.0010 the multiplier is 0.05, and a single step covers 95% of the distance. Just past that, at 1 ÷ 948, it is zero. Raise the rate further and the multiplier goes below zero.

Predict first

Set η to 0.0015. The multiplier is 1 − 948 × 0.0015 = −0.42, and the first step carries the guess from 1 past the answer, to 1.87. What happens after that?

It swings and settles.

The minus sign puts the guess on the other side of the answer at every step. The 0.42 still shrinks the distance, to 42% of what it was. The figure below is now playing that run. Going past the answer is not failing, as long as each overshoot is smaller than the last.

What the slope cannot see

A rule that follows the slope downhill ought not to end up higher. It does because of what a slope is. At a guess of 1 the slope is −577.5: for each unit the guess rises, the loss falls by 577.5. Take that at its word for a whole step at η = 0.0022, which is a step of 1.27. The loss should fall by 577.5 × 1.27, about 734. But the loss at 1 is only 175.9, and an average of squares cannot go below zero.

The slope was never a promise about a step that long. The previous page measured it with moves of 0.001, and it is exact only about the ground where the guess stands. A distance of 1.27 away, the loss has already reached its lowest point and turned upward. A small step stays where the slope's description holds. A big step lands somewhere the slope did not describe.

Predict again

At η = 0.0020 the multiplier is −0.90, and the run swings widely but settles. Raise the rate by a tenth, to 0.0022. What happens?

Each swing grows.

The multiplier is 1 − 948 × 0.0022 = −1.09, so every step leaves the guess 9% further from the answer than it was, on the other side. The figure below plays that run. It starts 0.61 from the answer and after twenty steps is away, with a loss that rises at every step. One tenth more turned a program that learns into one that gets worse the longer it runs.

Every rate at once

One run tells you about one rate. Step up a level: run the program at every rate from 0.0001 to 0.0025, and count the steps each run takes to settle. Settling here means the previous page's stopping rule, a step smaller than 0.0001.

The dots make a valley with one gentle side and one cliff. From the left, raising the rate helps: steps at 0.0001, at 0.0005, at 0.0010. Past 1 ÷ 948 the multiplier is negative and the count climbs again, because the swings take longer to fade: steps at 0.0015, at 0.0020. At 0.0021 the multiplier is −0.99 and the run needs over a thousand steps. At 2 ÷ 948, about 0.00211, the multiplier reaches −1, and past it no number of steps is enough.

The two sides are not alike. A rate that is too small costs time, and doubling it helps. A rate that is too large loses everything, and the fastest rate is exactly half of the first one that fails.

One trip moved the cliff

The cliff sits at 2 ÷ 948, so everything depends on the steepness. It comes from the pulls on the previous page. A trip's pull is 2 × miles × miss, and its miss is its miles times the guess's distance from the answer, give or take the rounding in the log. So each trip contributes 2 × miles² for each unit of distance, and the steepness is twice the average of miles squared.

Predict once more

With the seven trips, the largest rate that settles is about 0.0021. Add the 150-mile trip from the top of the page. Where is the largest settling rate now?

The cliff is now at 0.00031.

Squared, 150 miles is 22,500, almost seven times the other seven trips' squares put together. The average of miles squared jumps from 474 to 3,227, the steepness from 948 to 6,455, and the cliff from 2 ÷ 948 to 2 ÷ 6,455. The figure below shows every rate again with the trip in the log: only the three smallest still settle.

At 0.0005, which sat comfortably in the valley, the multiplier is now 1 − 6,455 × 0.0005 = −2.23. That is the table at the top of the page, and the run the figure below opens on. Nothing was wrong with the trip or with the rule. The ground became steeper under a step sized for gentler ground.

Where this stops matching real training. One weight scored by squared misses has the same steepness at every guess, so a rate that settles at the start settles all the way. A real model's loss is steeper in some places than in others, so the safe rate changes as the weights move. Real training usually changes the rate as it goes: the models in Kaplan et al., 2020 (§2.2) raised it over the first 3,000 steps and then lowered it toward zero. A real model also has many weights sharing one rate, each on ground of its own steepness, which is the next page. And real training computes its slopes on a fresh batch of examples at each step, so they are noisy estimates rather than exact.

Try it without the page

Two trips: 10 miles logged as 16 km, and 20 miles as 32 km. What is the steepness of their loss? Which learning rate reaches the answer in one step, and what is the largest rate that still settles?

Miles squared are 100 and 400, with an average of 250, so the steepness is 500 and each step multiplies the distance by 1 − 500η. That is zero at η = 1 ÷ 500 = 0.002, which lands in one step. The run settles while the multiplier stays between −1 and 1, so for any rate below 2 ÷ 500 = 0.004. You can check the one-step rate from a guess of 1: the slope is the average of 2 × 10 × (10 − 16) and 2 × 20 × (20 − 32), which is −300, and 0.002 × 300 is a step of 0.6, straight to 1.6.
