Learning from examples

350 billion tries, or one

The loss can say more than how wrong a guess is. Read the right way, it says which way to move, and roughly how far.

The program on the previous page learned km per mile by trying each nudge both ways: score the guess a little higher, score it a little lower, keep the better one. That costs two scorings for its one weight on every step, and a scoring means running the model on every example.

GPT-3 has 175 billion weights (Brown et al., 2020). Trying each one both ways would take 350 billion runs of the whole model for a single step of training, and training takes many thousands of steps. Real training does each step for about the cost of three runs: one to score the examples, and about two more to work out which way every weight should move (Kaplan et al., 2020, §2.1).

Those two extra runs compute the loss's slope at the current guess: how much the loss changes for each unit the guess moves. A negative slope means the loss falls as the guess rises, so the guess should go up; a positive slope means it should come down. Across many weights, the list of slopes is called the gradient, an old word for how steeply a road or railway climbs, from a Latin root meaning a step (Etymonline).

This page works it out for the one weight from the previous page, km = w × miles, on the same seven trips, and then lets the slope steer.

Measure it first

The plainest way to find a slope is to measure it. Move the guess a little, see how much the loss changes, and divide by how far you moved. Start from the guess w = 1, where the loss is 175.867.

A move of 0.1 gives −530. A move of 0.01 gives −573, and 0.001 gives −577. The answers stop changing as the move shrinks, settling near −577.5. That settling is what makes the slope a single number at a single guess. It happens because the loss is built from multiplying and squaring, which bend smoothly: look closely enough and any smooth curve looks straight, and a straight line has one steepness.

Measuring still costs a scoring per weight, though. The useful part is that nobody has to measure.

Each trip pulls

Follow one trip through a tiny move. If the guess rises by a sliver, the trip's prediction, w × miles, rises by miles times that sliver, and so does its miss. A miss of −10 that grows by 0.01 becomes −9.99, and its square drops from 100 to 99.8001: a change of about 2 × miss × 0.01. Squares always change that way under small moves, because the sliver squared is too small to count.

Put together, each trip changes the loss by about 2 × miles × miss for every unit the guess moves. Call that the trip's pull. The loss is an average over trips, so its slope is the average pull:

slope = average of 2 × miles × (w × miles − km)

That reads: a trip pulls hard when it misses by a lot, and harder still when it is long, because on a long trip every bit of the guess is multiplied by many miles.

The average pull is −577.5, the number the measurements were closing in on, and it took no extra scoring. The misses were already in hand from scoring the guess once; the pulls are one multiplication each. A real model with billions of weights does the same bookkeeping backwards through its layers, giving every weight its slope from the same single scoring. That procedure is called backpropagation, after the paper that made it widely known (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986); it is where the two extra runs go.

Step against the slope

The slope says which way: against its sign. It also suggests how far. A simple rule, and the one real training starts from, is to step a fixed fraction of the slope:

new w = w − 0.0005 × slope

That reads: move against the slope, by 0.0005 of it. At w = 1 the slope is −577.5, so the first step is +0.289, up toward the answer. The 0.0005 is a fixed number picked to suit these trips; how to pick it is the subject of the next page.

Predict first

Every step is 0.0005 times the slope, wherever the guess happens to be. The first one moves the guess from 1 to about 1.29. What happens as the guess nears 1.609?

The steps shrink by themselves.

Take the steps below and watch the step size column. Nothing in the rule says to slow down near the answer, and it slows down anyway.

From 1, the steps going up are 0.289, 0.152, 0.080, 0.042: each a bit over half the one before, and each covering about 47% of the distance still left. After nine steps the guess is 1.607; the size of a step tells you how far there is to go.

The reason is in the pulls. Every pull is 2 × miles × miss, and every miss shrinks in step with the guess's distance from the answer, so the slope does too. For these trips the slope is about 948 times the distance, with the sign pointing back toward 1.609: −577.5 at 1, where the guess is 0.609 short, and −103 at 1.5, where it is 0.109 short. The loss of a single weight scored by squared misses always behaves this way. Plotted against the guess it would make a parabola, a symmetric bowl, though neither you nor the program ever sees it. So a step of 0.0005 × slope is a step of 0.474 × distance: big when far, small when near, and never planned.

Let the program step

The program follows the rule without being asked which way: it computes the slope, steps against it, and stops when a step would be smaller than 0.0001.

Predict again

A start of 4.0 is about four times as far from 1.609 as a start of 1. From 1 the program takes steps. How many does it take from 4.0?

Only a couple more.

From 4.0 it takes steps. Each step removes about 47% of whatever distance is left, so four times the distance costs only the two steps it takes to shrink it fourfold. The far starts take giant first steps: from 4.0 the first one is 1.13.

Where this stops matching real training. A model with many weights and layers does not have a perfect bowl for a loss, so the slope is proportional to the distance only close to the bottom; far away, a steep slope does not always mean a long way to go. The fixed 0.0005 was picked for these trips. Longer trips make the slope steeper, and the same fraction would overshoot, which is where the next page begins. And real training computes its slopes on a fresh batch of examples at each step, so they are noisy estimates rather than exact.

Try it without the page

Two trips: 2 miles logged as 3.2 km, and 4 miles as 6.4 km. With the guess w = 1.5 and squared misses, what is the slope? Take one step of 0.01 × slope: where does the guess land?

The predictions are 3.0 and 6.0 km, so the misses are −0.2 and −0.4. The pulls are 2 × 2 × −0.2 = −0.8 and 2 × 4 × −0.4 = −3.2, and their average, the slope, is −2.0. The step is −0.01 × −2.0 = +0.02, so the guess lands at 1.52. The answer here is 1.6: the step closed 0.02 of the 0.1 gap, and the next step, with a smaller slope, will close a bit less.
